The green suite lie

Two test suites. The same fifteen lines of code under test. Both green, both at one hundred percent line coverage. One of them catches three of the twelve ways that code can break, and the other catches eleven. Coverage cannot tell them apart, and coverage is what your dashboard shows you. Tonight you run both suites yourself, in this page, and watch the metric that actually distinguishes them.

LiveWhy this course is not a tour of testing tools3 min▶

There is good official training on AI test generation. Microsoft Learn has an eight-unit module on generating unit tests with Copilot. LinkedIn Learning has an hour and seventeen minutes on AI across the testing lifecycle. Playwright has genuinely impressive test agents. This course maps against all of them honestly at the bottom of every session.

What none of them teaches is how to tell whether the tests you just generated are any good. That question has real research behind it, the answer is not coverage, and it changes how you work.

Real world

The most dangerous artifact in software is a test suite that is green, fast, at high coverage, and checks almost nothing. It is dangerous precisely because every dashboard you own reports it as healthy, and because it will keep reporting that right up until something ships.

Part 1 · covers what green actually proves

A passing suite is weaker evidence than it looks

6 min live

Start with the humbling numbers, because they reframe everything after them. Google ran the largest published study of continuous testing on their own corpus, and the shape of the result is not what most engineers expect.

LiveThree numbers to carry for the rest of the course4 min▶
  • 91.3 percent of test targets never failed once. They ran, they passed, and they never once told anybody anything they did not already know.
  • 1.23 percent ever caught a developer-introduced breakage. After filtering out the flakes, that is the fraction of the suite doing the job the suite exists for.
  • About 84 percent of pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test. When your suite goes red, the base rate says it is probably not a real regression. Google also measures a continual 1.5 percent of all test runs reporting a flaky result, and almost 16 percent of their tests have some level of flakiness.

None of this means testing does not work. It means a green run and a red run are both weaker signals than the colour suggests, and that the interesting question is not "did it pass" but "would it have noticed."

Part 2 · covers coverage versus effectiveness

Coverage asks whether a line ran, never whether you checked it

7 min live

This is the load-bearing idea of the whole course, and unusually for this subject it is settled research rather than opinion.

Self-studyThe evidence, with citations you can hand to a sceptic6 min▶

Three sources, and they agree.

★ The paper that settles it Inozemtseva and Holmes, "Coverage Is Not Strongly Correlated with Test Suite Effectiveness", ICSE 2014. ACM Distinguished Paper. 31,000 generated test suites across five systems of up to 724,000 lines. Fault detection measured with mutation testing. "there is a low to moderate correlation between coverage and effectiveness when the number of test cases in the suite is controlled for", and "stronger forms of coverage do not provide greater insight". Conclusion: coverage "should not be used as a quality target because it is not a good indicator of test suite effectiveness."

Google says the same thing in plainer language, and note the careful asymmetry, because it is why you should still measure coverage: "A high code coverage percentage does not guarantee high quality in the test coverage." But "a low code coverage number does guarantee that large areas of the product are going completely untested."

Their published bands are 60 percent acceptable, 75 percent commendable, 90 percent exemplary, and they warn that "the gains of increasing code coverage beyond a certain point are logarithmic".

Martin Fowler on the hundred percent smell: "high coverage numbers are too easy to reach with low quality testing", and 100 percent "smells of someone writing tests to make the coverage numbers happy, but not thinking about what they are doing." He quotes Brian Marick, and this is the line to remember: "I expect a high level of coverage. Sometimes managers require one. There's a subtle difference."

What to do with coverage, then. Keep it as a floor detector, not a quality target. Low coverage genuinely tells you something is untested. High coverage tells you nothing at all about whether it is checked.

Self-studyWhat does correlate: assertions4 min▶

The companion finding, and it points the other way. Zhang and Mesbah, ESEC/FSE 2015, built 6,700 test suites from 24,000 assertions across five real Java projects and found that "the number of assertions in a test suite strongly correlates with its effectiveness", and that "assertion coverage is strongly correlated with effectiveness".

Their premise line is the one sentence that explains the entire course: "coverage without checking for correctness is meaningless."

Google's own mutation-testing paper puts the mechanism precisely: coverage "might be misleading, in particular when program statements are covered but the expected program outcome is not asserted upon." That sentence describes exactly what you are about to watch happen.

Build-along

Run both suites, then kill a mutant

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The playground below is not a simulation. Your tests are compiled and executed in this page, the coverage is counted from lines that actually ran, and the mutants are real edits to the source, recompiled and re-run against whatever you write. Every number is a real result.

Start on the weak suite and run it. One test, one assertion, green. This is close to what a generator hands you when you ask for a test and accept the first answer.

Switch to the coverage tab. One hundred percent. Every line of the function is green. On most dashboards this module is now considered done.

Switch to the mutation tab. Twenty-five percent. Nine of the twelve real changes to that code went completely unnoticed by your suite. Read the survivor list, because each row is a bug that could ship today with the suite still green.

Now load the strong suite and repeat all three tabs. Same code. Same one hundred percent coverage. Mutation score 91.7. Nothing on a coverage dashboard would ever have distinguished these two.

Find the one survivor. The strong suite still misses a mutant: memberSince < 2020 becomes <= 2020. The suite tests 2019 and it tests 2021. It never tests exactly 2020, and the boundary is where bugs live.

Kill it yourself. Add this line to the editor and run the mutation tab again. It passes against the real code and fails against the mutant, which is precisely what a good test does.

★ The test that kills the last mutant test("member joined exactly in 2020", function () { eq(price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2020), 108); });

Watch it go to one hundred percent, zero survivors. You just did, by hand, what a coverage number could never have told you to do.

Now break something on purpose. Change an expected value to a wrong number and run it. The failure message is real. This playground has no way to fake a pass.

The honest limit, said now rather than later. Mutation testing is a better proxy than coverage. It is still a proxy. In the paper that validated it, for 95 of 357 real faults, roughly 27 percent, no mutant was coupled to the real fault at all. Session 4 covers where it does and does not help.

Homework

Before session 2

25 min
  1. Pick the module. Something you own, small enough to read in one sitting, important enough that a silent bug would matter. That module is the artifact for all six sessions.
  2. Write down its current coverage. Whatever your tooling says. You will want the number later, mostly to stop caring about it.
  3. Run a mutation tool on it once. Stryker for JavaScript or TypeScript, PIT for the JVM, mutmut for Python. Do not tune anything. Just look at the first survivor list and read three entries.
  4. Find one real survivor and write the test that kills it. One. That single test is worth more than the next ten percent of coverage.
  5. Count your assertion-free tests. Search your suite for tests that call something and never assert on the result. Write down how many. Session 2 is about that number.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Two suites both hit 100 percent line coverage on the same function. What does that tell you about their relative quality?

You watched it live: 100 percent on both, mutation scores of 25 and 91.7. Inozemtseva and Holmes found only a low to moderate correlation between coverage and effectiveness once suite size is controlled for.

2 · A mutant survives your test suite. What does that mean?

A survivor is a real edit to the source that nothing noticed. It is not a bug in your code, it is a hole in your tests, and it is the most actionable signal in this course.

3 · Google publishes coverage guidelines of 60, 75 and 90 percent. Why do they not recommend chasing 100?

Their words: "The gains of increasing code coverage beyond a certain point are logarithmic." Fowler puts it more bluntly: 100 percent "smells of someone writing tests to make the coverage numbers happy."
