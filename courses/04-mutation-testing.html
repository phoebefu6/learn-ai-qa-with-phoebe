Mutation testing, done properly

In session 1 you killed a mutant with your hands and took a score from 91.7 to 100. You did not learn what a mutant is, where the idea comes from, what the research actually proved, or why the naive version of this tool is unusable on a real codebase. Tonight is all four, including the part most write-ups leave out: the twenty-seven percent of real faults that mutation testing cannot reach.

Part 1 · covers how mutation testing works

The tool breaks your code on purpose and asks who noticed

5 min live

A mutation tool takes your source, applies one small syntactic edit, recompiles, and re-runs your suite against the changed version. Each edited copy is a mutant. The edits are not random noise. They are drawn from a fixed catalogue of operators, and every one of them is a change a tired human being could plausibly have written.

LiveKilled, survived, and the one sentence that matters4 min▶

The bookkeeping is simple enough to explain to a sceptical manager in thirty seconds. Killed: at least one test failed when the mutant was in place, so your suite noticed the change. Survived: the mutant was compiled, the whole suite ran, and every test still passed. Mutation score: killed divided by total mutants generated, a percentage that looks exactly like coverage, which is exactly why people misuse it like coverage.

Say this out loud every time you teach it. A survivor is not a bug in your code. Your code is fine. A survivor is a hole in your tests: proof that somebody could make that precise edit to that precise line, ship it, and every gate you own would stay green.

That reframing is the whole reason mutation testing beats coverage as a working metric. Coverage hands you a number about lines. A survivor list hands you a stack of concrete, addressable, reproducible statements of the form "this change would go unnoticed". You can act on the second one this afternoon.

Self-studyWhy the score itself is the least useful part of the output3 min▶

Two teams both report a mutation score of 71 percent. One has thirty survivors clustered in a payment calculation. The other has thirty survivors spread evenly across logging, string formatting and defensive branches nothing depends on. The number is identical and the situations are not remotely comparable. This is the same trap coverage sets, one level up. Treat the score as a direction of travel and the survivor list as the artefact. When a team asks what score to aim for, the honest answer is that the question is already off track: read the survivors on the code you changed this week and decide, one by one, whether you would ship that edit.

Part 2 · covers the validation study

The evidence that mutants stand in for real faults

4 min live

The obvious objection is the right one to raise first. Killing an artificial edit is not the same as catching a real bug, so why should anyone care? Because somebody went and measured it against real faults, on real applications, and published the result.

LiveThe paper, and the clause that does the work4 min▶

This is the strongest empirical result in the whole course, and it is worth quoting precisely rather than paraphrasing, because the last four words are the ones a sceptic will try to take away from you.

★ The validation study Just, Jalali, Inozemtseva, Ernst, Holmes and Fraser, "Are Mutants a Valid Substitute for Real Faults in Software Testing?", FSE 2014. 357 real faults across five applications, totalling 321,000 lines. "The results show a statistically significant correlation between mutant detection and real fault detection, independently of code coverage." 73 percent of real faults were coupled to mutants generated by common operators.

"Independently of code coverage" is the load-bearing phrase. It is not saying that suites which kill more mutants also happen to be bigger, and bigger suites cover more, and that is why they catch more bugs. The correlation survives once coverage is accounted for. Mutation detection is carrying information that coverage does not have.

Put next to session 1's finding, the pair is unusually clean for software research. Inozemtseva and Holmes: coverage has only a low to moderate correlation with effectiveness once suite size is controlled for. Just et al.: mutant detection correlates with real fault detection independently of coverage. Same research community, same era, opposite verdicts on the two metrics.

Self-studyHow to answer "these are fake bugs" in a review3 min▶

You will hear it, usually from the most experienced person in the room, and it is a fair instinct. The answer has three moves and none of them require you to overclaim. Concede the premise: yes, a mutant is a fabricated edit, and nobody is arguing it is a real defect. Point at the coupling result: somebody checked whether killing the fabricated ones predicts catching the real ones, on 357 genuine faults, and it does, independently of coverage. Then concede the ceiling before they find it: 27 percent of those real faults were not reachable this way at all. Part 3 is that number, and offering it yourself is what makes the first two moves credible.

Part 3 · covers the honest limit

The twenty-seven percent that mutation testing cannot reach

4 min live

Teach this in the same breath as the validation, not in a footnote three weeks later. The moment a metric gets sold as complete is the moment it starts doing the same damage coverage did, and this course does not have the standing to make that mistake twice.

LiveWhat lives in the unreachable 27 percent4 min▶

The same paper that validated the technique also bounded it, and the sentence is unambiguous.

✗ The number the vendor slide never shows "for 95 out of 357 real faults (27%), none of the triggering tests detected any additional mutants." And for 63 of those 95, no obvious mutation operator could generate a coupled mutant at all.

Read that second part carefully. It is not saying the tool was configured badly or that a richer operator set would have caught them. For 63 real faults, there was no obvious single-token edit that stands in for what actually went wrong. Whole categories of defect are shaped that way: a missing feature, an entire branch that was never written, a specification misunderstood at design time, a wrong algorithm implemented flawlessly.

The sentence to carry. Mutation testing is a better proxy than coverage. It is still a proxy. A mutation score of 100 percent means every edit your tool knows how to express would be noticed. It does not mean your software is correct, and it never claimed to.

Which is why this course keeps insisting on the thing underneath both metrics: tests written from the specification, asserting on behaviour a human agreed was correct. No automated proxy substitutes for having decided what the code is supposed to do.

Part 4 · covers making it usable

Run it on the diff, and suppress without mercy

5 min live

Everything so far argues that you should do this. Part 4 is why almost nobody does, and what the largest deployment on record had to change before their own engineers would tolerate it.

LiveThe scale, and the number that saved the programme5 min▶

Google published their production mutation-testing system, and the honest part of the paper is the failure that came first.

  • Two billion lines of code and around 500,000,000 tests run daily. This is the largest continuous testing environment anyone has written up.
  • 16,935,148 generated mutants, with the system deployed to more than 24,000 developers.
  • And the sentence that nearly ended it: developers initially rated 85 percent of mutants unproductive. Only fifteen in a hundred were worth a human glance.

The fix was not a better algorithm. It was suppression heuristics, built from six years of feedback from over 20,000 developers, that refuse to surface mutants in places where a surviving mutant almost never means anything: logging, unreachable defensive code, statements whose alteration cannot change observable behaviour. That moved the productive ratio from 15 percent to 89 percent, and cut a changelist from a median of 820 mutants to 7. In production, 82 percent of surfaced mutants that received feedback were labelled productive.

★ The transferable lesson, and it is the whole of Part 4. Run mutation testing on the diff, not on the codebase, and suppress aggressively. A tool that shows a developer seven relevant mutants at review time gets used. The same tool showing 820 gets switched off in a fortnight and never turned back on.

Self-studyChange-detector tests, and why the same paper names them3 min▶

Chasing a mutation score without judgement pushes you straight into a failure mode Google names in that paper: tests whose "change-detector nature (specifically testing the current implementation rather than the specification) violates testing best practices and causes brittle tests and false alarms."

The mechanism is easy to fall into. A mutant survives, you want it dead, so you write a test asserting whatever the current implementation happens to do at that line. The mutant dies, the score goes up, and you have added a test that fails on every legitimate refactor and catches nothing. You have converted a hole in your tests into a tax on future changes.

The discipline is the one from session 2. Kill a survivor by asserting on behaviour someone specified. If you cannot state what the correct behaviour at that line is without reading the implementation, that survivor belongs in the suppression list, not in a new test.

What to install, in one minute

That is the difference between due diligence and gossip, and it is the same habit this course applies to research claims.

Build-along

Run it on your diff and kill three real survivors

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Not on your codebase. On the diff of whatever you changed most recently in the module you have carried since session 1. If you have no recent change, make a small one first. The point of tonight is to feel what this tool is like when it is scoped the way Google had to scope it.

Run on diff. Point Stryker, PIT or mutmut at the changed files only. Whole-codebase runs are how this tool gets abandoned.

Triage. Go down the survivor list once and sort each into two piles: a hole a human would care about, or noise. Do not fix anything yet.

Kill. Take three from the first pile and write the test the specification implies. Assert on behaviour, never on the current implementation.

Suppress. Everything in the second pile goes into the config with a one-line reason. An unsuppressed noise mutant reappears every single run.

Scope it to the diff. Stryker has incremental and since-commit modes, PIT has scmMutationCoverage, mutmut takes explicit paths. Whatever your tool calls it, find that flag before you run anything, not after.

Run it once and time it. Write the wall-clock number down. If it is longer than your coffee, this will never live in your review loop and you need a narrower scope, not more patience.

Read the survivor list top to bottom without fixing anything. This is the discipline most people skip. You are triaging, and triage stops working the moment you start treating.

Sort every survivor into real hole or unproductive. The test is one question: would you sign off a pull request that made exactly this edit? Yes means unproductive. No, or "wait, what" means real.

Kill three real ones. Boundary survivors are the best starting material because the fix is unambiguous: test the boundary value itself, not the values either side of it.

Suppress the rest, with a reason each. A suppression with no reason is indistinguishable from a suppression that was wrong, and in six months you will not be able to tell them apart.

★ The shape of a test that kills a boundary survivor // Survivor: line 14, ConditionalBoundary, qty < 10 became qty <= 10 // The suite tests qty of 9 and qty of 11. It never tests exactly 10. test("quantity of exactly 10 does not get the bulk rate", function () { eq(price([{price: 20, qty: 10}]), 200); }); // Passes on the real code. Fails on the mutant. That is the definition // of a test worth having, and the reason to write this one rather than // asserting on whatever the function returns today.
Real world

The most common way this fails on a real team is not disagreement about the evidence. It is that somebody runs it on the whole repository, gets four hundred survivors, feels the whole thing is hopeless, and quietly removes it from the build two sprints later. Google needed a median of seven before 24,000 engineers would use it. Start where they ended up.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Wire the diff-scoped run into something you can repeat. A make target, an npm script, one line in a README. If running it requires remembering a flag, you will stop running it.
  2. Write your suppression list, with reasons. One line each. This file is the honest record of what you decided not to care about, and it is more useful in six months than the score ever will be.
  3. Kill two more survivors from the specification. Before you write either test, say out loud what the correct behaviour is. If you cannot say it without opening the implementation, suppress it instead and note why.
  4. Find one fault in your own history that mutation testing could never have caught. A missing branch, a misread requirement, an entire feature nobody built. Write one sentence about it. That is your personal copy of the 27 percent.
  5. Time your full suite and write the number down. Then find the single slowest test in it. Session 5 opens on that test, and on why it is probably also your flakiest.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A mutant survives your suite. What have you learned?

Your code is fine. A survivor is a hole in your tests, and that is precisely what makes it actionable in a way a coverage percentage never is.

2 · Just et al. found a significant correlation between mutant detection and real fault detection. What is the phrase that makes the result strong?

Without that clause the result could just be that bigger suites cover more and catch more. With it, mutant detection is carrying information coverage does not have. It is also why the paper answers session 1's coverage finding so directly.

3 · Google's mutation system was initially rejected by its own users. What fixed it?

Developers rated 85 percent of mutants unproductive. Six years of feedback from over 20,000 developers moved the productive ratio from 15 percent to 89 percent. The technique never changed, only what it chose to show you.
