Flakes and speed

A suite nobody trusts is a suite nobody reads, and flakiness is how that trust dies. Not in one dramatic incident, but over about six weeks of red builds that turned out to be nothing, until the team learns that red means rerun. The numbers on this are published, they are large, and one of them tells you exactly which test to go fix tonight.

Part 1 · covers the scale of the problem

Red usually does not mean broken

5 min live

Start with the definition, because half the arguments about flaky tests are people using the word differently. Google's is precise and unarguable: a flaky test is "a test that exhibits both a passing and a failing result with the same code." Same commit, same inputs, two answers. Then the numbers, from the largest published continuous testing environment anybody has written up.

LiveThe three numbers, and the one that reframes everything4 min▶

You met these in session 1 as a warm-up. Tonight they are the working material, so slow down on the third one.

  • A continual rate of about 1.5 percent of all test runs reporting a flaky result. Small as a percentage, enormous as an absolute number at any scale, and it never goes to zero.
  • Almost 16 percent of tests have some level of flakiness. Not sixteen percent of failures. Sixteen percent of the suite, carrying some non-determinism.
  • About 84 percent of the transitions from pass to fail involve a flaky test. Read that again with your own dashboard in mind. Roughly five in six times a green thing goes red, a flake is in the picture.

Why this is a trust problem before it is a technical one. If five out of six red builds turn out to be nothing, a rational engineer learns to rerun before reading. That habit is correct on the numbers and catastrophic on the sixth build. Flakiness does not just waste time, it trains your team to ignore your suite, and it trains them well.

Self-studyFlaky is a property of the test, not of the day3 min▶

The definition rules out the two most common excuses. It says with the same code, so "the environment was weird" does not make a test not flaky. That is exactly what flakiness is: sensitivity to something that is not the code under test. Infrastructure, clocks, ordering, network, available memory, whatever it happens to be.

It also rules out the reverse move. A test that fails once and passes on rerun has demonstrated both results on the same commit. It is flaky now, by definition, whether or not you can explain it yet. Not being able to reproduce it is a fact about your tooling, not evidence of innocence.

The practical consequence is that "flaky" is a label you can apply immediately and confidently on one observation. You do not have to win a diagnosis argument first, which matters, because the diagnosis argument is where flaky-test work goes to die.

Part 2 · covers what actually predicts flakiness

Flakiness scales with test size

5 min live

This is the finding that turns a vague complaint into a work item, and it is the single most useful thing in tonight's session. Google looked for what predicts a test being flaky, and the answer was not the framework, the language or the team. It was how big the test is.

LiveWhat this means for a generated test suite specifically5 min▶

Take the three numbers seriously as a ratio, not just as a ranking. Small 0.5 percent, medium 1.6, large 14. Moving one test from large to small is not a marginal improvement in reliability, it is close to two orders of magnitude.

Google's supporting evidence is that binary size correlates with flakiness at R-squared 0.82 and memory at 0.76, and their own conclusion is blunt: "size is more predictive than tool". Whatever framework you migrate to, you carry your flakiness with you if the tests stay the same shape.

Real world

Here is where this collides with the subject of this course. Ask a model to "write a test for the checkout discount rule" without constraining it, and there is a good chance you get a browser test: launch the app, click through to checkout, add an item, read the total off the page. It is a perfectly reasonable-looking test. It is also large by every definition Google uses, and you have just bought a fourteen percent flakiness rate to verify a piece of arithmetic that a small test would have pinned down deterministically in four milliseconds.

The rule to take back to your team. AI that reaches for a browser test where a unit test would do is buying flakiness by construction. Not by writing bad code, not by hallucinating, simply by choosing the wrong size. So constrain the size in the prompt, and review generated tests for size before you review them for correctness. Size is the cheaper thing to check and the more expensive thing to get wrong.

Self-studyChoosing the size deliberately, every time3 min▶

None of this says browser tests are bad. It says they are expensive in a currency most teams do not price, and that the price should be paid on purpose. Some behaviour genuinely only exists in a real browser, and for that behaviour a large test is the right instrument.

The question to ask of every test, generated or hand-written, is simply: what is the smallest test that could still catch this? If the answer is smaller than what you have, you are paying a flakiness premium for nothing. Ask it during review, while changing the answer is cheap. Asking it six months later means a rewrite, and rewrites of tests are the work that never gets prioritised.

Part 3 · covers what flakiness does to your signal

The part of the suite doing the work is smaller than you think

4 min live

Session 1 opened on this funnel as a provocation. Tonight it is evidence for something specific: flakiness does not merely add noise on top of a good signal, it corrupts the strategies you would otherwise use to manage a large suite.

LiveThe consequence they drew, in their own words4 min▶

The funnel is striking, but the operational finding underneath it is the one to take home. Once flakes make up most of your recently-failing tests, the obvious optimisation stops working.

✗ The heuristic that stops working Memon et al., ICSE-SEIP 2017, on 5,562,881 test targets: 91.3 percent never failed once. 2.07 percent both passed and failed at some point. Of those, 46,694 were flakes. Only 1.23 percent ever caught a developer-introduced breakage. "we could not rely on regression test selection heuristics such as 'rerun tests that failed recently' as we would end up mostly re-running flaky tests."

"Rerun tests that failed recently" is a sensible instinct and it is what almost every hand-rolled test-selection script does. The finding is that in a suite with real flakiness, that heuristic mostly selects your flakes, because flakes fail more often than real regressions do. You have built a system that spends its budget re-running the tests least likely to tell you anything.

Which closes the loop with Part 2. Flakiness is not an annoyance sitting alongside your test strategy. It is an input to your test strategy, and if you do not measure it, the optimisations you layer on top will quietly point in the wrong direction.

Part 4 · covers documented mitigations

What to actually do, from teams who published their answer

5 min live

There is a great deal of folklore about fixing flaky tests. This section sticks to mitigations that a named team has written down and put its name to, which turns out to be a short and unglamorous list.

LiveRetries as a diagnostic, never as a fix5 min▶

Playwright ships the most clearly specified retry model of the mainstream runners, and its definition is worth adopting whatever tool you use: a flaky test is one that "failed on the first run, but passed when retried". Results land in three buckets rather than two, and retries are configurable globally, per file, or per group.

Note what that gives you that a plain rerun does not. A silent rerun turns a flake into a pass and the information disappears. A runner that reports "flaky" as its own outcome turns every rerun into a measurement, and measurements accumulate into a list of your worst tests without anyone doing extra work.

The line to hold in review. Retries are a diagnostic that buys you time. They are not a fix. Turning retries up until the suite goes green is the single fastest way to arrive at a suite that is always green and means nothing, which is the same failure this course opened with, reached by a different road.

Google's own framing of the marking mitigation is worth borrowing too: a test marked flaky reports failure only after failing three times in a row. That is honest about what it is. It is not claiming the test is fixed, it is trading a defined amount of sensitivity for a defined amount of noise, and writing the trade down where everyone can see it.

Self-studyQuarantine, done in a way that does not rot3 min▶

Quarantine is the most useful of these and the easiest to do badly. Done well it means: the test is pulled out of the blocking run, a bug is filed automatically, and the quarantine list is visible and small. Done badly it means a test was disabled once, nobody filed anything, and two years later nobody remembers whether that behaviour is covered at all.

Two rules keep it honest. Every quarantined test has a ticket, and the quarantine list has a length somebody is embarrassed by. Google pairs quarantine with automated monitoring and bug filing precisely so the decision leaves a trace. A quarantine with no trace is just a deletion with extra steps.

The other piece of their list that most teams never build is tooling to identify the submission that changed a test's flakiness. It sounds exotic, but the cheap version is within reach: record the flaky outcome with a commit, and you can at least bisect when a test starts misbehaving instead of arguing about it.

Part 5 · covers brittle selectors

The biggest single source in generated UI tests

3 min live

Short section, and pointed at one specific habit. When a model writes a browser test, it very often reaches for whatever selector uniquely identifies the element in the DOM it is looking at right now. That is how you get a locator built out of nested div positions, and it is why the test fails the week someone adds a wrapper.

The vendor's own reasoning, which is the useful part. Playwright says to prefer user-facing attributes because "Your DOM can easily change so having your tests depend on your DOM structure can lead to failing tests". A role, a label or visible text is part of what your product promises a user. A div path is an implementation detail that nobody agreed to keep stable, so a test built on one is a change-detector wearing a browser.

★ The constraint to put in the prompt, before the request Write this as the smallest test that can still catch the bug. Prefer a unit test; only use a browser test if the behaviour genuinely requires a real browser. If it must be a browser test, locate elements by role, then text, then label, then placeholder, alt, title or test id. Do not use CSS or XPath selectors. Do not add waits with fixed durations.
Build-along

Find your flakiest test and deal with it tonight

★ 22 min · everyone builds

One test. Not a strategy document, not a backlog epic. You are going to find the worst offender in the suite you brought, classify it by size, and leave with it either genuinely smaller or deliberately quarantined with a ticket. Both outcomes count as finishing.

Find. Whatever history you have: CI reruns, the test everyone reruns by reflex, the slowest test you timed after session 4. Start there, they are usually the same test.

Classify. Small, medium or large. Does it touch a real browser, a real network, a real database, another process? Each yes moves it up a size.

Decide. Shrink it, or quarantine it on purpose. Deciding not to decide is what leaves it failing intermittently for another year.

Record. A ticket if quarantined, a note on what got smaller if shrunk. Untraced decisions become somebody else's mystery.

Pick your candidate in two minutes, not twenty. If you have flaky reporting, sort by it. If you do not, ask the person next to you which test they rerun without reading. That answer is reliable and takes ten seconds.

Classify it by size honestly. Count what it touches outside its own process: a browser, the network, a real database, the filesystem, a clock, another service. Zero of those is small. One or two is medium. Anything more is large and comes with the fourteen percent.

Ask the shrinking question. What is the smallest test that could still catch the bug this test exists to catch? Very often the answer is a small test on one function plus a much thinner browser test that only checks the wiring.

If it is a browser test, check its locators. Any CSS or XPath selector goes on the list to replace with a role, text or label locator. This is frequently the entire fix, and it takes minutes.

If you cannot shrink it tonight, quarantine it deliberately. Out of the blocking run, ticket filed, name on the ticket. That is a decision with a trace, and it is far better than leaving it to fail one build in six.

Write down what it was costing. Reruns per week times minutes per rerun, plus the builds people ignored because of it. You will want that number when you ask for time to do the next five.

★ What you walk out with tonight. One test that is smaller or one test that is quarantined on purpose, the size classification that tells you which of the others to do next, and the number that justifies the time. That is a flaky-test programme. Everything else is a wiki page.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Classify your whole suite by size. Rough counts are fine: how many small, medium, large. This takes twenty minutes and it is the map you will use for the next year.
  2. Turn on flaky reporting as its own outcome. If your runner supports a flaky bucket, enable it. If it does not, log reruns somewhere. You cannot manage what gets swallowed by a silent retry.
  3. Grep your browser tests for CSS and XPath locators. Count them. Convert the three in your most-rerun test to role, text or label locators.
  4. Write your team's retry policy in one paragraph. How many retries, reported how, and the explicit statement that a retry is a diagnostic and not a fix. One paragraph beats an unwritten habit.
  5. Bring three numbers to session 6. Your suite's wall-clock time, your rough flaky rate, and your survivor count from session 4. Session 6 turns those three into a gate you can defend in front of people who do not enjoy tests.
