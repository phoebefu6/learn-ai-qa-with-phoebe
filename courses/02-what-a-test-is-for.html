What a test is for

A test that executes code and asserts nothing is not a test. It is a smoke alarm with the battery out: mounted on the ceiling, visible from the doorway, reported as present by every inspection, and silent when the room fills with smoke. Tonight you go through the suite you brought and count how many of those you own. Then you rewrite three of them so they check behaviour at a boundary, which is the one thing the research says actually correlates with catching bugs.

Recap

Where session 1 left you

Two suites, the same fifteen lines under test, both at one hundred percent line coverage, mutation scores of 25 and 91.7. Coverage could not tell them apart. Tonight is the answer to the obvious follow-up question: what was the good suite doing that the weak one was not?

🎫 Clear all three quiz questions to stamp your passport Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class

★ What you walk out with tonight. A count of the assertion-free tests in your own suite, a count of the change-detector tests, and three rewritten tests that assert on behaviour at a boundary. Also a one-sentence habit that quietly fixes the rest of them.

LiveThe one-sentence answer, said now so the rest lands3 min▶

The strong suite asserted. That is the whole difference, and it is not a slogan, it is a measured finding across five real Java projects.

Coverage counts the lines your test walked past. It has no opinion about whether you looked at any of them. A test can call your function, ignore the return value entirely, finish without throwing, and register as full coverage of every line it touched. That test tells you the code does not crash on one input. It does not tell you the code is right, and it will keep not telling you that forever.

Real world

Almost every suite has a layer of these. They usually arrive in one of three ways: a test written to satisfy a coverage gate, a test whose assertion was commented out during a flaky patch and never restored, or a generated test that called the function and checked only that it returned something.

Part 1 · covers the assertion finding

Assertions are the signal that coverage is not

6 min live

Two papers, similar scale, similar method, opposite conclusions. Put them side by side and the shape of the answer becomes obvious.

LiveThe finding, in the authors' own words5 min▶
★ The companion paper to session 1 Zhang and Mesbah, "Assertions Are Strongly Correlated with Test Suite Effectiveness", ESEC/FSE 2015. 6,700 test suites built from 24,000 assertions across five real Java projects. "the number of assertions in a test suite strongly correlates with its effectiveness", and "assertion coverage is strongly correlated with effectiveness". Their premise line is this session's thesis: "coverage without checking for correctness is meaningless."

Read that premise line twice, because it is doing more work than it looks. It is not saying coverage is useless. It is saying coverage measured on its own, with no account of whether anything was checked, is not a measurement of quality at all. Coverage plus assertions is a picture. Coverage alone is a headcount of who walked through the room.

Google's mutation-testing paper states the mechanism precisely: code coverage "might be misleading, in particular when program statements are covered but the expected program outcome is not asserted upon." That is the assertion-free test, described by an engineering organisation that runs 500,000,000 tests a day and had to build something better than coverage to survive it.

Self-studyWhy the two findings do not contradict each other4 min▶

People sometimes read these two papers as a fight. They are not. They are two halves of one sentence.

  • Coverage is about reach. Which parts of the system did any test touch. This is a real question with a real use: Google's own guidance keeps it because "a low code coverage number does guarantee that large areas of the product are going completely untested."
  • Assertions are about scrutiny. Of the parts you touched, how many did you actually inspect the outcome of. That is the part that correlates with catching bugs.
  • You need both, and only one of them is on your dashboard. Reach without scrutiny is what the green suite lie is made of. Scrutiny without reach is a beautifully tested corner of a system you are not looking at.

The reporting change worth making this week. Alongside your coverage number, publish an assertion count and a count of tests with zero assertions. It takes one search across the suite, and it is the first metric you have ever put on the dashboard that the research actually backs.

Part 2 · covers the anatomy of a real test

A test earns its place by answering three questions

6 min live

Not every assertion is worth having, so "add assertions" is not the lesson. The lesson is a short interrogation you run on each test, and each layer of it is a harder question than the one below.

LiveThe habit: write down what this test would catch5 min▶

One sentence per test, written next to it, in the form "this fails if someone ___". Not a description of what the test does. A description of the bug it stands guard against.

  • "This fails if someone applies the member discount to a non-member." Good. Specific, behavioural, and you could hand it to a person who has never seen the code.
  • "This fails if someone changes the price calculation." Bad, and it is bad in an interesting way. It is a description of the implementation, not the behaviour, and it is the exact tell you will meet in part 3.
  • "This tests the price function." Not a sentence about a fault at all. When this is the best you can write, the test has no answer, and a test with no answer should be deleted rather than kept.

Deleting tests feels wrong the first few times. It is not. A test with no answer to that question costs you build minutes, review attention and a false sense of safety, and returns nothing. The only thing it protects is the coverage number, and you spent session 1 learning what that is worth.

Where to put the sentence. The test name. If the name reads "this fails if someone applies the member discount to a non-member", the sentence is already written, it shows up in the failure output, and nobody has to maintain a comment.

Self-studyAt which boundary, and why the boundary is the whole game4 min▶

Session 1 ended on a mutant that survived a good suite: memberSince < 2020 becoming <= 2020. The suite tested 2019. It tested 2021. It never tested exactly 2020, so both versions of the code passed and the difference was invisible.

That is not an unlucky example, it is the normal shape of the problem. Every conditional in your code draws a line, and a test placed comfortably on either side of the line proves only that the line exists somewhere in the neighbourhood. A test placed exactly on it proves where.

✗ Near the boundary, which is not the boundary test("member discount applies", function () { eq(price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2019), 108); }); Passes on memberSince < 2020. Also passes on <= 2020, on < 2021, and on several other wrong versions of the same line.
★ At the boundary, where the bugs live test("member who joined exactly in 2020 still gets the discount", function () { eq(price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2020), 108); }); Passes on the real code and fails on the mutant. One test, and it pins the line to the exact year the specification says.

The pattern generalises past numbers. Empty list and one-element list. The first request and the request that trips the rate limit. The string of exactly maximum length. Zero, and the thing just before zero. If your specification contains a threshold, your suite should contain a test sitting on it.

Part 3 · covers change-detector tests

The other failure: tests that assert far too much

6 min live

The assertion-free test checks nothing. Its mirror image checks everything, including things nobody promised, and it makes your suite worse in a way that is harder to see because it is loudly, constantly red.

LiveChange-detector tests, and the tell that finds them5 min▶

Google named the category in their mutation-testing paper. Their words, and note that this is a description of a defect, not a style preference: tests whose "change-detector nature (specifically testing the current implementation rather than the specification) violates testing best practices and causes brittle tests and false alarms."

The tell is mechanical and you can apply it today. If you have to update a test every time you refactor without changing behaviour, it is pinned to the implementation, not to the specification. Rename a private method, extract a helper, swap a loop for a map, reorder two independent calls. Behaviour unchanged. If tests go red, those tests were never testing behaviour.

Three shapes that come up again and again:

  • Asserting on internal calls. "The service called the repository once with these arguments." Nobody promised that. The promise was about the value that came back.
  • Snapshot everything. A whole serialised object compared byte for byte, so adding an unrelated optional field turns the suite red and somebody updates the snapshot without reading it.
  • Asserting the exact wording of a message. Real if the wording is contractual and shown to users. A change detector if it is a log line.
Real world

The cost is not the red build, it is what the red build trains people to do. A suite that goes red on harmless refactors teaches its own team that red means "somebody moved something", and after enough repetitions the genuine failure arrives and gets the same shrug and the same snapshot update.

What it does. Calls price with a basket and a year. Finishes without throwing. Asserts nothing about the number that came back.

Coverage contribution. Every line of the function, marked green.

What it would catch. A crash. That is the complete list.

The sentence you can write next to it. None. This is the deletion signal.

What it does. Calls price with a two-item basket and the year 2020, and asserts the total equals 108.

Coverage contribution. Every line of the function, marked green. Identical.

What it would catch. A wrong discount, a wrong threshold, a wrong rounding, a wrong total.

The sentence you can write next to it. "This fails if the member threshold moves off 2020."

Build-along

Audit the suite you brought

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the module you picked in session 1 and its tests. Two counts and three rewrites. Nobody sees your numbers, which is exactly why they are worth writing down honestly.

Read. One test. Ignore the name, read the body. Find every assertion in it, and if there are none, mark the test.

Ask. Write the sentence: "this fails if someone ___". If you cannot finish it in one line, the test has no answer and it is a deletion candidate.

Assert or delete. Either add an assertion on the behaviour at the boundary the spec names, or remove the test. Do not leave it in the middle.

Re-run. Then break the code on purpose and re-run. A rewritten test that cannot go red is still not a test.

Count one: assertion-free tests. Search the suite for test bodies with no assertion call at all. Include the ones whose only assertion is that nothing threw. Write the number down, and write the total test count next to it so you have a ratio rather than a number you can argue with.

Count two: change-detector tests. Faster than it sounds. Do one harmless refactor in the module, rename a private helper or extract a block, and run the suite. Every test that goes red is a change detector, and you did not have to judge a single one by hand.

Pick three tests to rewrite. Not the easiest three. Take one assertion-free test, one change detector, and one test you have quietly never trusted.

Rewrite at the boundary. For each, find the threshold in the specification and put the assertion exactly on it. The value you assert should come from the specification, not from running the code and copying what it printed.

Prove each rewrite can fail. Change the code to be wrong on purpose, run, watch it go red, put the code back. A test you have never seen fail is a test you have never verified.

Undo the refactor from count two. Or keep it, if it was an improvement. Just do not leave the suite half-red overnight.

✗ Three tests that will be in your suite tonight test("price works", function () { price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2019); // asserts nothing }); test("returns a number", function () { ok(typeof price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2019) === "number"); }); // asserts almost nothing test("calls the discount helper once", function () { ok(spy.callCount === 1); // change detector });
★ The same three, rewritten to check behaviour at a boundary test("this fails if a member who joined in 2020 loses the discount", function () { eq(price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2020), 108); }); test("this fails if a non-member is given the member discount", function () { eq(price([{price: 60, qty: 2}], 2021), 120); }); test("this fails if an empty basket is not free", function () { eq(price([], 2019), 0); });

How to read that number. A test you cannot take in at a glance is usually testing more than one thing, and when it fails you will not know which. If a single test runs long, the fix is almost never a comment. It is two tests, each with its own sentence, each able to fail on its own.

Homework

Before session 3

25 min
  1. Finish the audit on the whole module. Not the whole repository. The one module you picked in session 1, every test it has.
  2. Delete the tests with no answer. The ones where you could not finish "this fails if someone ___". Commit the deletion on its own, with the reason in the message, so it is reviewable as a decision rather than hidden in a refactor.
  3. Rename every surviving test to its sentence. This is the highest value-per-minute task in the whole course. The names become documentation and the failure output starts telling you what broke instead of which function was involved.
  4. Write the specification down. A short plain-language description of what the module promises: inputs, outputs, thresholds, error cases. Half a page. Session 3 needs it, and you will find writing it changes at least one test.
  5. Record two numbers. Assertion-free tests before and after. Bring both.

Do not skip item 4. Session 3 is built on the difference between a test written from a specification and a test written from the finished code, and there are two independent studies saying that difference is worth roughly double. You will need the specification in your hand to see it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Zhang and Mesbah built 6,700 suites from 24,000 assertions. What did they find?

Their words: "the number of assertions in a test suite strongly correlates with its effectiveness", and "assertion coverage is strongly correlated with effectiveness". Set against session 1's low-to-moderate correlation for coverage, that is the whole argument of this course.

2 · You refactor a module without changing any behaviour and eleven tests go red. What have you learned?

Google's term for exactly this: tests whose "change-detector nature (specifically testing the current implementation rather than the specification) violates testing best practices and causes brittle tests and false alarms." Behaviour did not change, so a behavioural test should not have moved.

3 · A test calls your function and asserts nothing. Your coverage tool reports the lines as covered. What should you do with it?

Google's mutation paper on why coverage misleads: statements "are covered but the expected program outcome is not asserted upon." If you cannot write the sentence "this fails if someone ___", the test has no answer and it should go rather than stay.
