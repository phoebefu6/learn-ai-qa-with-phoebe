The quality gate

You already know coverage is the wrong gate. That was session 1, and the evidence has only piled up since. Tonight is the part that actually changes your Monday: deciding what the right gate is, picking numbers you can defend, and writing it into CI in a form that survives contact with a busy team. Part 1 · covers what to gate on

Four signals, and not one of them is a percentage of lines

6 min live

A gate is a promise about what cannot get in. Coverage makes a promise nobody can keep, because a line running is not a line being checked, and Inozemtseva and Holmes said so in the plainest possible terms: coverage "should not be used as a quality target because it is not a good indicator of test suite effectiveness". Everything you have measured over five sessions collapses instead into four signals that are cheap to compute and hard to fake.

LiveThe four signals, with starting numbers5 min▶

These numbers are starting points, not laws. Pick them, run them for a month, and move them based on what they actually catch and what they actually cost you. A threshold nobody has tuned is a threshold nobody believes.

SignalStart atWhy that number
Mutation score on the diff60 percentLow enough to pass on day one, high enough that an assertion-free test cannot reach it. Raise it 5 points a quarter until it hurts, then stop.
Assertion presenceEvery testZhang and Mesbah found the assertion count strongly correlates with effectiveness. This is the cheapest check you own.
New assertion-free testsZeroOld ones can stay. The gate is about what enters, otherwise nobody can merge until a backlog is cleared and the gate gets switched off.
Flakiness budget1.5 percent of runsGoogle's measured continual rate. If you are well above it you have a signal problem before you have a coverage problem.

Gate on the diff, not the codebase. This is the single decision that makes the whole thing survivable. Google's mutation work took a changelist from a median of 820 mutants down to 7 by filtering hard, and 7 is a number a reviewer will read. 820 is a number a reviewer will mute.

Real world

A gate you can pass by executing code without checking it rewards exactly the tests you least want. The failure mode is never that someone games it on purpose. It is that a generator produces forty tests, they all execute code, the coverage bar goes green, everyone is relieved, and nobody looks again. Google's own mutation paper names the mechanism: coverage "might be misleading, in particular when program statements are covered but the expected program outcome is not asserted upon".

Part 2 · covers keeping coverage, demoted

Coverage keeps its job, it just stops being the target

6 min live

Deleting the coverage report is the wrong move, and Google's own wording says why. The claim is asymmetric: a high number proves nothing, a low number proves something real. Keep the half that works.

LiveThe demotion, in three sentences you can say in a standup4 min▶

One: coverage stays on the dashboard and stays reported, because "a low code coverage number does guarantee that large areas of the product are going completely untested". Two: it stops being a merge condition, because "a high code coverage percentage does not guarantee high quality in the test coverage". Three: the merge condition moves to mutation score on the diff, which measures whether a change would be noticed.

If someone wants a coverage rule anyway, give them the floor rather than the target: alert when a module drops below 60, and never celebrate a module for reaching 90. Google's own note is that "the gains of increasing code coverage beyond a certain point are logarithmic", which is a polite way of saying the last stretch is where the worthless tests live.

Self-studyThe closing argument, for the meeting where someone wants 100 percent4 min▶

Fowler's line does the work: 100 percent "smells of someone writing tests to make the coverage numbers happy, but not thinking about what they are doing", and "high coverage numbers are too easy to reach with low quality testing".

He quotes Brian Marick, and this is the sentence to end on, because it separates the two people in the room without insulting either: "I expect a high level of coverage. Sometimes managers require one. There's a subtle difference."

The practical version of that distinction is a config change: coverage keeps reporting and gains an alert below 60, and the line that says fail_under moves off coverage and onto mutation score. Same file, same amount of CI, a completely different promise.

Part 3 · covers making it survivable in CI

Mutation testing survives CI only if you shrink it first

6 min live

Every team that abandons mutation testing abandons it for the same two reasons: it took too long, and the survivor list was full of mutants nobody cared about. Both are solvable, and the solutions are measured rather than folklore.

LiveFour moves that make the run fit inside a pull request5 min▶
  • Changed files only. Mutate the lines in the diff. Nothing else. This is the difference between a run measured in hours and one measured in minutes, and it is the same choice Google made at two billion lines.
  • Cache what did not change. Mutation results for untouched files are still valid. Keep them keyed on file hash and reuse them for the nightly summary.
  • Set a wall-clock budget. Give the PR run a fixed ceiling. When it runs out, report what it got and pass with a note rather than blocking on a timeout, because a gate that flakes is a gate that gets removed.
  • Suppress the unproductive operators. When a survivor is genuinely not worth killing, record why and stop generating it. Google moved the productive ratio from 15 percent to 89 percent doing exactly this. Unproductive mutants are feedback about your operator set, not evidence against the technique.

Then split the work in two. The pull request job is a gate: small, fast, blocking, scoped to the diff. The nightly job is instrumentation: full suite, full mutation, coverage floors, flake counts. It never blocks, because nobody can act on a red build they woke up to. Put the flake list in the nightly output specifically, since Google's finding that flakiness scales with test size, 0.5 percent small, 1.6 percent medium, 14 percent large, means that is where you catch a suite drifting towards big slow tests before the gate starts failing for reasons nobody can reproduce.

The trap to avoid. Do not answer a noisy survivor list by lowering the score threshold. That keeps the noise and removes the signal. Suppress the operator, keep the bar.

Part 4 · covers the AI-specific gate

Generated tests arrive in volume, which makes the gate matter more

7 min live

Nobody hand-writes forty tests in an afternoon, so nobody ever needed an automatic answer to "is this test worth having". Generation removes that constraint, and the review capacity that used to be sufficient stops being sufficient. Meta already solved this, and published the shape of the answer.

LiveThe rule: never merge a generated test that does not demonstrably improve the suite5 min▶

Meta's system "verifies that its generated test classes successfully clear a set of filters that assure measurable improvement over the original test suite, thereby eliminating problems due to LLM hallucination". Read that twice. The defence against a hallucinated test is not a better prompt and not a human skimming a diff. It is a filter that runs and either passes or does not.

Their numbers are the reason the filter has to be automatic. 75 percent of generated test cases built at all. 57 percent passed reliably. 25 percent increased coverage. Of the recommendations that survived all of it, 73 percent were accepted by engineers. A one-in-four hit rate is genuinely useful and completely unreviewable by hand at volume.

★ The gate rule for generated tests. A generated test merges only if it clears every filter automatically: it compiles, it passes on repeated runs, and it kills at least one mutant the existing suite did not already kill. Anything that fails a filter is discarded silently. Nobody negotiates with it.

Self-studyWiring the filter chain into your own pipeline5 min▶

You do not need Meta's infrastructure to copy the shape. Four checks, in order, cheapest first.

★ The filter chain, as a merge condition 1. Does it build? discard if not 2. Does it pass 3 runs of 3? discard if not (flake filter) 3. Does it kill a mutant the existing suite missed? discard if not (the real one) 4. Does it assert on a value? discard if not Survivors go to a human. Everything else is deleted, uncounted, and never appears in a pull request.

Check three is the one that carries the weight, and it is the one people leave out because it costs a mutation run. It is also the only check in the list that can tell a useful test from a test that merely runs. The other three are hygiene.

Real world

A generated test that passes, asserts, and kills nothing new is worse than no test. It adds runtime to every future CI run, it adds a file someone will have to maintain, and it makes the suite look larger and stronger than it is.

Part 5 · covers what green is allowed to mean

Green means the tests ran, and your gate decides the rest

5 min live

Back to where session 1 started, now that you can do something about it. The numbers have not changed. What has changed is that you no longer need the colour to carry weight it cannot carry.

LiveWhat green is now allowed to mean4 min▶

Green means the tests ran and none of them complained. That is the whole claim, and it was always the whole claim. Of 5,562,881 Google test targets, 91.3 percent never failed once, and only 1.23 percent ever caught a developer-introduced breakage. Red is not much stronger, since about 84 percent of pass-to-fail transitions involve a flaky test.

Your gate is what makes green mean more. Green plus a mutation score on the diff means a change was made and something would have noticed if it were wrong. Green plus an assertion check means nothing entered the suite that only executes code. That is a claim worth defending in a release meeting, and the colour on its own never was.

Build-along

Write the gate for the repository you actually work in

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Open the CI config for the module you have carried through six sessions. You are writing four things: what runs on a pull request, what runs nightly, what blocks a merge, and what happens when it blocks the wrong thing. Twenty-two minutes is enough because the decisions are already made.

Push. The diff is the unit of work. Nothing outside the changed files is in scope for this run.

Diff scan. Assertion check first, because it costs milliseconds. Any new test with no assertion on a value fails here.

Mutate. Mutation operators on the changed lines only, with a wall-clock budget and the cache warm.

Judge. Score against the threshold. The survivor list goes in the pull request comment, and it is short enough to read.

Merge. Pass, or block with a named override. No third option, because a soft gate teaches people to ignore it.

Write down the three thresholds first. Mutation score on the diff, assertion rule, flakiness budget. Put them in a file in the repository, not in a CI dashboard, so changing them shows up in a diff and someone has to agree.

Split the jobs. One pull request job scoped to changed files with a hard time budget. One nightly job that runs everything and blocks nothing. If you only have time for one tonight, build the pull request job.

Run it against last week's merged pull requests. This is the step people skip and it is the one that saves the project. If your new gate would have blocked six of the last ten merges, the threshold is wrong, not the team.

Write the override, then the generated-test filter. The override is a label, a comment or a second approval, as long as it is visible and leaves a record. The filter is four checks: builds, passes on repeat runs, kills a new mutant, asserts on a value. Anything failing one never reaches a human.

Say what the gate does not do. Write one line in the README. It does not prove correctness, it does not replace review, and roughly 27 percent of real faults are coupled to no mutant at all. A gate that overclaims loses trust the first time something ships broken.

★ The whole gate, small enough to paste into a repository gate: on_pull_request: mutation: scope: changed-files min_score: 60 # starting point, review monthly time_budget: 8m # over budget reports, does not block assertions: new_tests_must_assert: true blocks_merge: true override: label "gate-override" + a comment saying why nightly: mutation: full coverage: report only, alert if a module drops below 60 flakiness: list every test that passed and failed on the same commit blocks_merge: false
The gate runs without anyone remembering to run it Automatic on every pull request, or it is not a gate.
A survivor on changed code has actually blocked a merge at least once An untriggered gate is a decoration. It has to have said no.
Nothing entered the suite this month without an assertion on a value Count them. Not "we usually do" - count them.
You can state your flakiness rate as a number, and it is inside your budget If you cannot name the number, the budget is not real.
Every generated test that merged was measured against the suite it joined Measured by a filter that runs, not by a person skimming the diff.
Homework

The last one: run it for a month, then tune it

30 min now, then a month of watching
  1. Turn the gate on in reporting mode for two weeks. It comments, it never blocks. You are collecting evidence about your thresholds, not enforcing them yet.
  2. Count two things every week. How many pull requests the gate would have blocked, and how many of those blocks you agree with. When the second number is most of the first, switch blocking on.
  3. Tune one threshold, once. Resist the urge to move all three. Move the mutation score by 5 points, or suppress one noisy operator, and see what a month does. Thresholds that move weekly are thresholds nobody plans around.
  4. Re-run session 1's playground with a full passport. Open the green suite lie again and run the weak suite. You will read the survivor list completely differently now, and that difference is the whole course.
  5. Write the paragraph. One paragraph for your team explaining what your gate checks and what it does not claim. If you can write it without mentioning a coverage percentage, you have finished the course.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and the final stamp goes in your passport.

1 · Why gate on mutation score of the diff rather than the whole codebase?

The technique was never the problem. The volume shown to a human was. Suppression heuristics moved the productive ratio from 15 percent to 89 percent, and 7 survivors is a list a reviewer will actually read.

2 · Meta's TestGen-LLM generated tests at industrial scale. What did the value actually come from?

Their words: the system "verifies that its generated test classes successfully clear a set of filters that assure measurable improvement over the original test suite, thereby eliminating problems due to LLM hallucination". Only 25 percent increased coverage, and the chain is what found them.

3 · If coverage is not the gate, why keep measuring it at all?

The asymmetry is the whole reason to keep it. Google: high coverage "does not guarantee high quality in the test coverage", but low coverage does guarantee untested areas. Floor detector, not target.
